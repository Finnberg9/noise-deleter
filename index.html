Background

Noise is a public health concern.

Noise pollution in professional and public environments reduces quality of life for people with sensory processing conditions and can cause permanent hearing damage for workers in construction, industrial facilities, and generator environments. Existing solutions have significant limitations. Passive barriers are ineffective against low-frequency noise, while consumer ANC headphones only protect a single listener and are impractical in many workplaces.

The Noise Deleter addresses this gap with a self-contained active noise cancellation system that creates a localized quiet zone using spatial sound cancellation. Built from commodity hardware and operating on less than 10 W of power, it provides targeted noise reduction without requiring users to wear headphones or modify the surrounding environment. Its modular design makes it suitable for construction sites, industrial facilities, care environments, and other applications where localized noise suppression can improve safety, comfort, and accessibility.

Sensory-Sensitive Individuals

Everyday noise in care facilities causes significant distress for people with sensory processing disorders.

Occupational Hearing Damage

Workers at construction and industrial sites are exposed to noise exceeding safe thresholds, causing permanent loss.

Outdoor Events and Music Festivals

No Practical Middle Ground

Existing solutions are either too limited (headphones, foam) or too complex and expensive for real deployment.

Design

System Architecture

The Noise Deleter consists of four integrated subsystems: acoustic sensing, digital signal processing, hardware control, and acoustic output. Environmental noise is captured using digital I²S MEMS microphones, providing feedback signals to the ESP32-S3 microcontroller, which executes the FxLMS adaptive noise cancellation algorithm in real time. The calculated anti-noise signals are transmitted through an I²S/TDM audio interface to eight independent Class-D amplifier channels, driving a phased speaker array to create destructive interference at the target listening position.

The system integrates power regulation, processing, audio interfaces, and speaker connections onto a custom PCB within a compact enclosure. The enclosure maintains precise speaker spacing to produce a directional acoustic beam while supporting microphone placement and internal electronics. Together, these subsystems form a closed-loop active noise cancellation system capable of adapting to measured acoustic conditions and reducing targeted noise levels.

Eight-Driver Speaker Array

Eight K36WP Drivers (36 mm) spaced 43 mm apart. At 2 kHz the array is ~1.5 wavelengths long, producing a beam half-power width of approximately 34 degrees.

FxLMS Adaptive Controller

One complex weight per speaker (8 total) updated every sample at 48 kHz. The FxLMS rule adjusts each weight to minimize the error microphone signal, scaled by the measured secondary path.

Fully Custom PCB

All amplifier, microphone, microcontroller, and power-supply circuits on one board. Careful trace and via design minimizes EMI between Class-D amplifier outputs and sensitive MEMS microphone inputs.

Closed-Loop Acoustic Feedback

Reference mic near the disturbance source and error mic at the listening position feed back into the adaptive controller via I²S, continuously driving residual error toward zero.

3D-Printed Enclosure

Custom enclosure designed in CAD and manufactured in-house. Houses the speaker array, PCB, wire routing channels, and a separate mount for the feedback microphone.

The Prototype

Built, for real.

From digital design to physical prototype, this process captures the complete development journey of the Noise Deleter. Beginning with CAD modeling and enclosure design, the system progressed through fabrication, electronics integration, and bench testing before becoming a fully assembled prototype. Key Findings

  • Secondary-path phase accuracy is critical — errors beyond ~90° cause divergence
  • I²S constraints limited design to one reference + one error microphone
  • System autonomously recovered from instability via divergence detection
  • Acoustic reflections and coupling were the primary performance ceiling

Future Improvements

  • Automatic frequency estimation — remove fixed 2 kHz assumption
  • Multi-tone simultaneous cancellation via concurrent adaptive filters
  • TDM-capable microphones for additional spatial error sensors
  • On-device secondary-path measurement to eliminate calibration errors
  • Adaptive beam steering to follow listener position in real time
